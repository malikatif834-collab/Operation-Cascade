II The Threat Environment

Agentic AI systems are currently operating across critical infrastructure. The Canada Cyber Centre, CISA, NSA, the United Kingdom, Australia, and New Zealand issued a coordinated advisory establishing this as present fact. Their consensus: the risks are real, and the immaturity of relevant standards is actively amplifying them.

Two immediate requirements follow from that consensus. The first is to expand threat intelligence through cross-sector collaboration. The second is to develop robust, agent-specific evaluations. Neither requirement has been met. Neither is close.

The scale of the problem compounds daily. Agentic AI in supply-chain software is expanding from under two billion dollars in 2025 to fifty-three billion by 2030. Sixty percent of supply-chain enterprises will have adopted agentic systems within five years. Multi-agent orchestration — agents directing other agents across organizational and jurisdictional lines — grew from one percent to twenty-two percent of deployments in just two years. Eighty percent of workers already use unapproved AI tools. Only eleven percent of those using generative AI at work do so through governed corporate channels.

The governance gap is not approaching.
It is already here, and the systems are already running.

The architecture of risk is layered. Enterprise platforms — SAP Joule, Oracle across twenty-two operational areas, Salesforce Agentforce in eighteen thousand organizations, Microsoft, Amazon, Google — sit atop orchestration frameworks that string multiple AI providers together, which in turn draw from a dense and largely ungoverned marketplace of skills and plugins. Agents operating across these tiers meet at connection points without any shared standard. Nobody designed the intersection. It emerged entirely from the speed of adoption.

The traditional breach lifecycle runs two hundred and seventy-seven days from intrusion to containment. That benchmark was established for human-operated attacks. Autonomous agentic propagation does not observe human timelines.

The legal architecture fails at precisely the moment it is needed. Under Canada’s federal privacy framework, transferring data processing to a third party does not transfer accountability — organizations remain legally responsible for their entire supply chain. Bill C-8 mandates fines up to fifteen million dollars and personal liability for directors and officers. Yet when an autonomous agent at a fourth-tier supplier makes a harmful decision that propagates through multiple intermediary suppliers before downstream harm occurs at a critical infrastructure operator, three questions have no answer: which regulatory body has standing, which framework governs liability, and who holds the duty of care when no human intent existed.

Voluntary industry protocols cannot resolve this. A 2025 technology consortium standard with over a hundred and fifty participating organizations establishes identity documents, basic authentication, and task tracking — and carries no enforcement mechanism. An accredited national standard, legally defensible and harmonized with international frameworks, capable of being mandated as a contract condition in government procurement and adopted by reference in regulation, does not yet exist.

NIST and the EU AI Act signal urgency.
Neither produces the enforceable standard this interaction requires.

IV The Response — Operation Cascade

Operation Cascade is the mechanism built to bridge the Five Eyes guidance and the standards that do not yet exist. It is a G7-level agentic supply-chain threat simulation that generates the empirical foundation necessary for a living, accredited national standard — through three interlocking pillars that the exercise is designed to produce simultaneously.

The first pillar is reproducible methodology. Scalable controlled adversarial scenarios, built on existing university research infrastructure and cloud environments, that can be replicated and expanded as the threat landscape evolves. The second pillar is empirical field data: not modeled risk, but documented behavior — technical propagation, human governance decisions, framework breakdowns, and jurisdictional friction captured during a live multi-sector exercise. The third pillar is the living standard itself: a legally defensible accredited national standard for Canada, built entirely on field-validated data, harmonized through the Digital Governance Standards Institute, ISO, and the OECD to serve as the G7 reference model.

An accredited standard, once issued, reorganizes market behavior immediately. Government agencies specify compliance as a contract condition; the standard becomes the market floor, enforced through every public sector RFP. Regulators across sectors adopt it by reference, building technical requirements into their frameworks without authoring them from scratch. Documented compliance becomes legal evidence of due diligence. And through Canada’s standards infrastructure, the exercise produces the international reference model that allied democratic economies need.

The methodology merges public mandate alignment with direct private sector incentives. The public sector stream utilizes existing research infrastructure and draws on mandate alignment across federal bodies for sustained funding as scenarios scale in complexity. The private sector stream targets organizations whose tools are being tested and whose customers will be governed by the standard. A third force accelerates both: liability surrounding agentic AI is forcing new underwriting models, and field-validated empirical data from this exercise carries immense actuarial value to insurance carriers.

  • National cybersecurity bodies — mandate to protect infrastructure
  • Government standards agencies — authority to set digital compliance metrics
  • Critical-infrastructure operators — managing the active supply chains
  • Top-tier academic researchers — intersection of technical execution and legal theory
  • International research partners — ensuring borderless relevance and harmonization
  • Provincial and municipal authorities — downstream nodes in the cascade

The Five Eyes guidance exists.
The standards do not.
Operation Cascade is the mechanism to bridge them.

V Earn Your Seat

The institutional authority required to execute this mandate is already assembled. No further authorization is required to begin. Participation in Operation Cascade is not open enrollment — it is reserved for institutions with operational standing in the sectors the exercise models. The Evaluation Committee considers each application against the three questions that follow.

  1. i

    Who are you?

    Your institution, your mandate, your stake in the problem. What sector do you operate in, and what is your operational relationship to agentic supply chains or the governance of critical infrastructure?

  2. ii

    What do you bring?

    Operational data. Research capacity. Simulation expertise. Governance authority. Procurement influence. What does your participation make possible that is otherwise impossible — and what does your absence leave unrepresented?

  3. iii

    Why does this matter to you?

    Not a credentials summary. Tell us what is at stake for your organization — and why you need to be in the room when this exercise runs and the standard is written.

Applications are reviewed by the Operation Cascade Evaluation Committee.
Selected participants are contacted directly. There is no waitlist.
